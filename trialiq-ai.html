CONTEXT

Clinical screening is a quiet bottleneck in a high-stakes workflow.

Clinical trials fail or stall for a lot of reasons, but one of the quietest is screening. Before a single patient is enrolled, coordinators have to cross-reference dense protocol criteria against scattered patient records — notes, labs, histories — spread across formats that were never designed to talk to each other. It's slow, manual, and easy to get wrong in ways that don't surface until much later.

AI is the obvious lever here. Language models are good at exactly this kind of matching and synthesis. But clinical decisions are not a place where "the model said so" is an acceptable answer — and most AI screening tools I looked at treated the model as a black box that outputs a verdict, not a collaborator that shows its work.

THE PROBLEM

The brief I set for myself wasn't "build an AI screening tool." It was:

How do you let AI meaningfully accelerate a high-stakes decision, without asking the person responsible for that decision to trust it blindly?

That reframing changed almost everything downstream — this isn't a speed problem, it's a trust and verification problem that happens to also need to be fast.

CONSTRAINTS

Three constraints shaped the product before the interface did.

01

No ground truth to design against.

Every trial's protocol is different, so the interface can't assume a fixed data shape — it has to stay legible across wildly different criteria sets.

02

The user is the accountable party, not the AI.

Any design that let the AI's output feel final (rather than proposed) was a non-starter — liability and clinical practice both demand a human sign-off.

03

Trust has to be earned per-decision, not once.

A single "the AI is usually right" moment of trust doesn't hold up in this domain — every individual match needs its own visible justification.

PROCESS

From workflow mapping to an evidence-grounded interaction pattern.

I started by mapping the actual screening workflow end-to-end — protocol interpretation, participant matching, lab/data review, and safety flagging — as one continuous task, not four separate tools stitched together, which is closer to how coordinators actually described their day.

From there, the core design question became: what does "showing your work" look like in an interface, not just in a model's output? I explored a few directions:

01

A chat-style AI assistant that answered screening questions conversationally — fast to build, but it reduced every decision to a single trust moment ("do I believe this answer or not"), which worked against the core goal.

02

A fully manual side-by-side comparison tool with AI-highlighted matches — safer, but too close to the status quo to save meaningful time.

03

The direction I landed on: an AI-suggested match, always paired in-line with the exact evidence it came from (protocol clause, lab value, clinical note), reviewed through an explicit confirm-or-flag action rather than a passive accept.

That third direction became the product's core interaction pattern.

KEY DECISIONS

Design choices that turn AI output into something a person can verify.

Evidence-linking over confidence scores.

Early explorations used a confidence percentage next to each AI match — common in AI product patterns, but it asks the user to trust a number instead of showing them why. I replaced it with a direct link from every suggested match back to its source evidence, so verification takes the same effort as reading, not investigating.

Review-and-confirm, not accept-and-move-on.

The AI proposes candidate matches; it never auto-advances a participant. Every match requires an explicit human action, and that action is logged — partly for accountability, partly so the interface never implies more autonomy than it actually has.

One workspace, not four tools.

Protocol criteria, participant data, and safety signals live in a single connected view instead of separate screens a coordinator has to mentally reconcile — reducing the chance that something gets missed at the seams between tools.

REFLECTION

In a high-stakes domain, some friction is the feature.

The hardest part of this project wasn't the AI — it was resisting the instinct to design for speed first. Every time I optimized a screen to remove a click, I had to ask whether that click was actually a moment of verification I'd be designing away. In a high-stakes domain, some friction is the feature.

If I were to push this further, the next problem is scaling explainability across trials with very different protocol structures without the evidence-linking pattern becoming inconsistent or noisy.
